About

अपना पुस्तकालय

What the library is, why it's needed, where the idea came from, and the person who started it.

What this is

Apna Pustakalay is a small, free library in Chopan, created through a personal initiative with the hope of building a shared space for reading and learning. It runs from a hall in a family home, with shelves along the walls, chairs for readers, and a quiet place where anyone can come, pick up a book, and spend some time reading.

It began with one person's own books and savings and has since grown into a space with a larger collection and a librarian to keep it running. It is not an NGO or an institutional programme; it is simply one person's effort to create something that can eventually belong to the wider community.

The name itself carries that idea: "Apna Pustakalay". Not a scheme delivered to people, and not charity given to them, but a library that people can use, care for, contribute to, and gradually make their own.

For now, it is one room in one home. What it becomes will depend on how many people choose to make it theirs.

What you won't find here

You won't find textbooks, exam guides, question banks, or anyone checking whether you've read enough to pass. Most of us have grown up with books that came with a test attached, and somewhere along the way reading started to feel like work, something we did for a mark, a grade, or someone else's expectations. This library isn't about preparing you for an exam; it's about making space for the curiosity that all that testing pushed aside.

What you will find is a quiet place to explore, where you can pick up a book simply because something caught your attention, discover new ideas, wander into unfamiliar worlds, and read at your own pace. There is no syllabus to follow, no test waiting at the end, and no pressure to finish what you start, so you can come in with a question, a little curiosity, or nothing at all, pick a book, turn a page, and stay as long as you like. All that's here are books, your curiosity, and the freedom to read because you want to.

What this library stands on

This is a safe space. Every child and every adult is welcome here regardless of caste, religion, gender or what their family earns. That is not a rule we invented. It is the promise the country already made to itself, and this library is one small room where it is kept.

Where this is, and why it is needed

Chopan is a small town in Sonbhadra, the southernmost district of Uttar Pradesh, where the state meets Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Bihar. The district covers 6,905 square kilometres and held a population of about 1.86 million at the 2011 Census. Industry is its main source of income — coal, power, cement — and very little of what it generates stays here.

More than a fifth of the district is Scheduled Caste, and slightly more than a fifth is Scheduled Tribe. Literacy across Sonbhadra stands at 64.03%. Split by gender, the gap is the whole problem: 74.92% of men are literate, and 52.14% of women.

Where the idea came from

The idea came from a book, first. In A Room of One's Own, Virginia Woolf argues that thinking is not free, it needs money, and it needs somewhere to sit. Early in the essay, she describes being stopped at the door of a college library because she is a woman and cannot enter unaccompanied. The library is right there, full of books, and yet it is not available to her.

In Chopan, there is no one standing at the door to keep a child out of a library. There simply isn't a library to walk into. The barrier is not a rule; it is an absence.

The idea of the library itself also seems to be disappearing. In many places, a library has come to mean a quiet room with rows of desks, where people arrive carrying their own books, notes or laptops and study for hours. There is nothing wrong with these spaces, but something is lost when a library no longer has books for you to discover. A library should be more than a place to sit and study. It should be a place where you can walk in without knowing what you are looking for, pick up a book, open it, and find something you did not know you wanted.

That is part of the reason for Apna Pustakalay. Woolf asked for a room of one's own. Here, the idea is to create a room of everybody's own, a free place where children can find books, sit down, read and encounter ideas beyond their classrooms and textbooks.

There is also a practical reason. Reading is one of the foundations of learning. A child who struggles to read has difficulty accessing almost everything else that school asks them to learn. But learning to read is only the beginning. The habit of reading has to be built and continually fed; without books around, that habit has little chance to take root.

This is something that Pratham's work brought into focus for me. Their experience with children showed the gap between being enrolled in school and being able to read with confidence. Their response was not only to teach children to read, but to create access to books and libraries that could help make reading a regular part of children's lives. The thinking was simple: if the habit is to last, books have to be within reach.

Apna Pustakalay begins from that same belief. The aim is not simply to put books on shelves, but to create a place where children can encounter them regularly, discover what they enjoy, and slowly make reading part of their lives. It is an attempt to fill an absence in Chopan, and to bring back the simple idea of a library as a room full of books, open to everyone.

Sources: Virginia Woolf, A Room of One's Own (1929); Pratham and Pratham Books; ASER; published reviews of community and school library evidence in India.

Ragini KashyapFounder, Apna Pustakalay

I grew up in Anpara, an hour down the road from here. My parents live in Chopan now, and the library is in their house. I went to Delhi University for a degree and to Jawaharlal Nehru University for a master's in International Relations, and I manage a research team in Delhi.

Leaving Sonbhadra was possible because of a few things that were not distributed evenly: parents who treated a daughter's education as non-negotiable, enough money for a train ticket, and books. Plenty of people I went to school with were as capable and had none of that.

I don't think of this library as paying a debt. I think of it as sharing a privilege. The difference matters. A debt gets settled and closed; a privilege shared only gets wider. What I had was books and the time to read them. That is the specific thing I can hand over, so that is what this is.

Most of the adult collection is my own — history, politics, education, India, world affairs, and the fiction I grew up on. It stays as a read-here collection rather than a lending one, because those are books I want intact when I move back. Eventually all of them will be on these shelves. They are more useful in a room people can walk into than in a flat in Delhi.

What this is meant to become

Apna Pustakalay is starting as a free community library in Chopan, Sonbhadra. Over time, we want the library to grow into a larger resource for learning about Sonbhadra, its history, people, culture, environment, economy and development. We want it to be useful to people who live here, to children and students growing up here, and to anyone who wants to learn about the district. As the library grows, so will its collection of books, documents and other resources.
