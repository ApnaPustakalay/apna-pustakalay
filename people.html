अपना पुस्तकालय

The founder, where the idea came from, and what the library is meant to become.

Photograph

Ragini KashyapFounder, Apna Pustakalay

I grew up in Anpara, about an hour down the road from here. My parents live in Chopan now, and the library is in their house. I went to Delhi University for my degree and Jawaharlal Nehru University for a master's in International Relations. I now manage a research team in Delhi.

Leaving Sonbhadra was possible because my family had the means to support my education, and my parents treated a daughter's education as non-negotiable. I also had access to books and the time to read them. Plenty of people I went to school with were just as capable and did not have the same opportunities.

I don't think of this library as paying a debt. I think of it as sharing a privilege. What I had was books and the time to read them. That is the specific thing I can share, so that is what this is.

Most of the adult collection is my own. It includes books on history, India, education, world affairs, politics, and fiction. They are being brought together here so that they can be read and used by people who walk through the door.

Where the idea came from

The idea came from a book, first. In A Room of One's Own, Virginia Woolf argues that thinking is not free, it needs money, and it needs somewhere to sit. Early in the essay, she describes being stopped at the door of a college library because she is a woman and cannot enter unaccompanied. The library is right there, full of books, and yet it is not available to her.

In Chopan, there is no one standing at the door to keep a child out of a library. There simply isn't a library to walk into. The barrier is not a rule; it is an absence.

The idea of the library itself also seems to be disappearing. In many places, a library has come to mean a quiet room with rows of desks, where people arrive carrying their own books, notes or laptops and study for hours. There is nothing wrong with these spaces, but something is lost when a library no longer has books for you to discover. A library should be more than a place to sit and study. It should be a place where you can walk in without knowing what you are looking for, pick up a book, open it, and find something you did not know you wanted.

That is part of the reason for Apna Pustakalay. Woolf asked for a room of one's own. Here, the idea is to create a room of everybody's own, a free place where children can find books, sit down, read and encounter ideas beyond their classrooms and textbooks.

There is also a practical reason. Reading is one of the foundations of learning. A child who struggles to read has difficulty accessing almost everything else that school asks them to learn. But learning to read is only the beginning. The habit of reading has to be built and continually fed; without books around, that habit has little chance to take root.

This is something that Pratham's work brought into focus for me. Their experience with children showed the gap between being enrolled in school and being able to read with confidence. Their response was not only to teach children to read, but to create access to books and libraries that could help make reading a regular part of children's lives. The thinking was simple: if the habit is to last, books have to be within reach.

Apna Pustakalay begins from that same belief. The aim is not simply to put books on shelves, but to create a place where children can encounter them regularly, discover what they enjoy, and slowly make reading part of their lives. It is an attempt to fill an absence in Chopan, and to bring back the simple idea of a library as a room full of books, open to everyone.

Sources: Virginia Woolf, A Room of One's Own (1929); Pratham and Pratham Books; ASER; published reviews of community and school library evidence in India.

What this is meant to become

Apna Pustakalay is starting as a free community library in Chopan, Sonbhadra. Over time, we want the library to grow into a larger resource for learning about Sonbhadra, its history, people, culture, environment, economy and development. We want it to be useful to people who live here, to children and students growing up here, and to anyone who wants to learn about the district. As the library grows, so will its collection of books, documents and other resources.
